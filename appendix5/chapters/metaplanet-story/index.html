The Metaplanet Story

Japan Tax Regime and Equity Wrapper Effects

In Japan, direct bitcoin gains have historically been treated as miscellaneous income under progressive tax rates, though this classification has been subject to ongoing review as policymakers consider aligning crypto assets more closely with financial securities, including recent proposals to regulate them under financial instruments frameworks.

In practice, bitcoin gains are classified as miscellaneous income (雑所得, zatsu shotoku), subject to progressive tax rates that can reach up to 55%, significantly higher than the flat taxation applied to financial securities.

Additionally, the NISA (Nippon Individual Savings Account) program allows qualifying equity investments to be held tax-advantaged within defined annual contribution limits, allowing gains within those limits to be realized tax-free. While bitcoin itself is not eligible for these programs, publicly traded equities are.

This has historically created a “wrapper effect”: investors can access bitcoin exposure indirectly through equity while benefiting from more favorable tax treatment and simplified reporting. As a result, capital that might otherwise flow into direct bitcoin ownership may instead be redirected into listed companies that hold bitcoin on their balance sheets.

This dynamic helps explain the emergence of firms such as Metaplanet, which has become a primary vehicle for bitcoin exposure within Japan’s public equity markets. These companies function as publicly traded bitcoin proxies, embedding exposure within familiar regulatory and tax frameworks.

More broadly, this illustrates a general principle: regulatory and tax regimes do not merely influence adoption—they shape the form in which adoption occurs. In jurisdictions where direct ownership is penalized or restricted, financial engineering adapts by embedding bitcoin exposure into equity, credit, or fund structures.

Over time, such wrapper effects may play a significant role in determining how institutional and retail capital enters the bitcoin ecosystem across different regions.

As tax treatment converges across asset classes, the relative advantage of equity-based exposure may evolve rather than disappear, increasingly shaped by factors such as access, structure, and capital-market integration. In this environment, firms must continuously adapt how they structure and deliver bitcoin exposure, refining instruments and capital formation strategies in response to changing investor preferences.

Moving Strike Warrants and Capital Formation Mechanics

Regulatory Context (4× Rule)

Japanese listed companies operate under regulatory constraints that limit total share issuance under a single approval framework—commonly referred to as the “4× rule,” which caps new issuance at roughly four times the company’s outstanding shares at the time of approval. Unlike U.S. at-the-market (ATM) programs, which allow continuous incremental issuance, Japanese firms often rely on structured warrant programs to raise capital.

Simplified Mechanics

1. A capital partner borrows shares from an existing large shareholder and sells them into the market, establishing a short position.

2. Simultaneously, the partner holds warrants allowing the purchase of newly issued shares at a formula-based price derived from recent trading levels (often linked to the prior close or a defined pricing window).

3. Upon issuance (typically T+2 settlement), the partner uses newly acquired shares to close the short position by returning shares to the lender.

4. The company receives capital from the warrant exercise and deploys it—typically for treasury expansion.

5. The economic incentive for the capital partner lies in the pricing difference between sale and warrant exercise, allowing the partner to capture the spread as compensation for providing liquidity and execution.

Numerical Example (Illustrative)

Assume the partner sells borrowed shares at ¥100 and later exercises warrants at a formula price of ¥98. The partner closes the short using the newly issued shares and captures a ¥2 per-share spread (before fees and execution costs), while the company receives the exercise proceeds as fresh capital.

Why This Structure Exists (4× Rule and Tranche Compliance)

Because the 4× rule constrains total issuance capacity, issuance is typically executed in sequential tranches. Each tranche settles before the next begins, allowing the issuer to remain compliant while spreading issuance over time rather than concentrating dilution into a single event. This sequencing also reduces prolonged market overhang by aligning issuance with prevailing liquidity conditions.

Pricing Dynamics and Volatility Dependence

Traditional moving-strike warrant programs in Japan often include exercise discounts in the range of 8–10%. However, tighter spreads are possible when the pricing formula references recent market prices.

In such structures, the partner’s economics are supported less by a fixed discount and more by volatility and trading liquidity. When shares exhibit sufficient intraday movement, the partner can recycle the trade across multiple tranches, capturing smaller spreads repeatedly.

This structure enables:

• Incremental capital formation

• Reduced prolonged market overhang

• Compliance with issuance limits

• Alignment with prevailing liquidity

Large-Scale Programs

Metaplanet’s “21M Plan” and subsequent “555 Plan” represent scaled implementations of this structure, significantly expanding its bitcoin holdings while remaining within regulatory boundaries.

Quantified Example: 21M Plan

In one widely referenced implementation, Metaplanet raised approximately ¥93.3 billion over roughly 60 trading days and expanded its bitcoin treasury from approximately 1,762 BTC to approximately 7,800 BTC, corresponding to an increase in reported bitcoin per share (BPS) of roughly 189% over the program period.

Structural Sensitivity

The model functions best during periods of:

• Strong trading liquidity

• Positive market sentiment

• Market value exceeding underlying asset value

During downturns, issuance may pause, and compression of market value relative to underlying value can render new issuance dilutive. Accordingly, disciplined pacing and liquidity management are essential.

When equity trades near or below underlying value, issuing shares can increase share count faster than treasury assets grow, reducing bitcoin per share and weakening the logic of accretive issuance. For this reason, issuers often slow or pause issuance during adverse conditions and rely on liquidity reserves and conservative capital planning until conditions improve.

System-Level Implication

Moving-strike warrant structures illustrate how regulatory constraints do not prevent capital formation, but reshape how it occurs. Rather than relying on continuous issuance, firms adapt by embedding issuance into structured, repeatable mechanisms that align with market liquidity.

In this way, capital formation becomes more dynamic and market-responsive—particularly when combined with bitcoin treasury strategies and balance-sheet–driven capital formation.

Term Sheet Overview: MARS and MERCURY Preferred Shares

MARS and MERCURY represent two complementary preferred equity structures designed to absorb different forms of capital within a bitcoin-backed capital structure. Together, they illustrate how traditional preferred equity can be adapted to function within a balance sheet anchored in a scarce monetary asset (bitcoin).

MARS (Class A Preferred) — Price-Stabilizing Income Instrument

MARS is senior to MERCURY and common equity and is non-convertible. Its defining feature is a dynamically adjusting dividend rate linked to its trading price.

When the instrument trades below par, the effective yield increases, making it more attractive to investors. When it trades above par, the yield moderates. This mechanism introduces a stabilizing feedback loop that helps anchor the instrument around its reference value.

Because of its seniority and price-stabilization design, MARS primarily targets income-oriented investors seeking predictable returns with lower volatility than common equity.

MERCURY (Class B Preferred) — Hybrid Income and Equity Participation

MERCURY is junior to MARS but senior to common equity. It offers a fixed cumulative annual dividend determined at issuance and includes a 1:1 conversion right into common shares at a predefined price.

This structure allows investors to receive predictable income while retaining optionality to participate in equity upside if the underlying stock performs strongly. If the conversion threshold is reached, holders may exchange their preferred shares for common equity, effectively transitioning from a fixed-income profile to a growth-oriented equity position.

As such, MERCURY bridges fixed income and equity, attracting investors who seek both yield and exposure to balance-sheet-driven appreciation.

Investor Segmentation and Capital Absorption

The distinction between MARS and MERCURY reflects deliberate investor segmentation:

• MARS → income-focused, price-stability-oriented capital

• MERCURY → hybrid capital seeking both yield and upside participation

This segmentation allows the issuer to access multiple pools of capital simultaneously without relying exclusively on common equity issuance.

Capital Authorization Framework and Scaling Capacity

MERCURY is authorized under an approved issuance framework permitting aggregate preferred issuance of up to approximately ¥555 billion. This authorization provides structured capacity for balance-sheet expansion while limiting reliance on dilutive common share issuance.

The first institutional issuance of MERCURY occurred in November 2025 through a third-party allotment, raising approximately ¥21.25 billion in gross proceeds.

Role Within the Bitcoin-Native Capital Stack

Within the broader capital structure, MARS and MERCURY function as complementary layers:

• MARS stabilizes capital and anchors yield-oriented demand

• MERCURY introduces flexible capital that can convert into equity during favorable

market conditions

Together, they enable capital formation across market regimes while preserving long-duration exposure to bitcoin on the balance sheet.

System-Level Implication

These instruments demonstrate how traditional financial structures—such as preferred equity—can be re-engineered around bitcoin as a collateral base. Rather than relying solely on operating cash flows or equity dilution, capital formation becomes increasingly tied to balance-sheet strength and asset appreciation.

In this way, instruments like MARS and MERCURY represent early examples of how bitcoin-native capital markets may evolve to integrate income, optionality, and monetary exposure within a unified framework.

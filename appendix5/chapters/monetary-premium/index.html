The Role of the Monetary Premium

Japan: Real Estate, Monetary Conditions, and Demographic Change

Japan provides a useful long-term case study of how real estate’s role as a store of value can shift under changing monetary and demographic conditions. Following the asset price bubble of the late 1980s, Japanese real estate values declined sharply and, in many regions, have not recovered to prior peak levels even decades later.

During the bubble period, declining interest rates, credit expansion, and strong economic growth contributed to rapid increases in land and property prices. Real estate functioned not only as a productive asset, but as a primary store of value and collateral base within the financial system.

In the decades that followed, however, persistent low interest rates alone proved insufficient to sustain property valuations. Demographic trends—particularly population aging and decline—reduced structural demand for housing in many areas. As a result, real estate in parts of Japan transitioned from a scarce, appreciating asset to one characterized by oversupply, regional divergence, and, in some cases, long-term price stagnation or decline.

This case highlights an important principle: the monetary premium embedded in real estate is not fixed. It depends on a combination of monetary conditions, credit availability, and demographic demand. When these factors shift, real estate may lose part of its function as a store of value and revert more closely to its underlying utility as a consumption or income-generating asset.

Land prices in Japan

Yet, exceptions like Japan, with its aging population and resulting housing surplus, caution against a universal application of this strategy. Demographic shifts in other regions could similarly challenge the long-term viability of real estate as a store of value, underscoring the necessity to consider broader economic and societal trends in valuation assessments.

However, notable exceptions exist, such as Japan, where unique cultural and demographic dynamics—especially an aging population leading to a housing surplus—mean real estate is not universally embraced as a store of value. This trend, already observable in rural areas of the Western world, suggests a potential shift in the long-term demand for real estate. The Japanese housing market's inability to recover to its peak levels from the 1990s serves as a cautionary tale. It highlights that similar demographic trends in other countries could lead to comparable challenges in maintaining real estate's value as a preferred store of value (17: Samuel, 2024).

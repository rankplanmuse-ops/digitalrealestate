The Strategy Playbook

Bitcoin-Backed Preferred Equity: Structure and Rationale

Bitcoin-backed preferred equity differs structurally from both traditional debt and common equity. Unlike bonds, which are serviced through operating cash flow, and unlike common equity, which represents a residual claim on profits, bitcoin-backed preferred instruments are supported primarily by balance-sheet assets—specifically, an overcollateralized bitcoin treasury.

This shifts the foundation of creditworthiness. Instead of relying on income generation, the structure depends on asset strength, balance-sheet flexibility, and continued access to capital markets.

Source of Yield

The yield associated with bitcoin-backed preferred equity does not originate from rents, operating profits, or business cash flow. Instead, it emerges from capital structure design and the interaction between fiat-denominated obligations and bitcoin’s monetary properties.

Distributions are funded through a combination of refinancing capacity, accretive capital issuance, and balance-sheet expansion against an appreciating collateral base. This creates a form of monetary spread, where the growth of the underlying asset exceeds the cost of capital.

Sustainability Conditions

The durability of this structure depends on two primary conditions.

First, the issuer must maintain access to capital markets to refinance obligations and issue new instruments when required. Without this access, the ability to sustain distributions becomes constrained.

Second, the long-term behavior of bitcoin as a monetary asset is critical. If bitcoin appreciates over time relative to fiat liabilities, the structure strengthens as obligations decline in real terms. If this dynamic weakens, the model becomes more reliant on external financing conditions.

Structural Comparison

Compared to traditional instruments, bitcoin-backed preferred equity occupies a hybrid position within the capital stack.

• Relative to bonds, it is less dependent on operating income and more dependent on

asset valuation.

• Relative to real estate or REIT structures, it does not rely on rental income or physical

asset performance.

• Relative to convertible instruments, it offers yield without requiring direct equity

participation, while still benefiting indirectly from balance-sheet expansion.

This positioning allows it to absorb demand from multiple segments of capital markets, including fixed income, hybrid securities, and yield-oriented investors.

Risk Considerations

The structure introduces a distinct risk profile that differs from traditional credit instruments.

• Refinancing dependency: The model relies on continued access to capital markets.

Periods of illiquidity or risk aversion can constrain refinancing capacity.

• Market access risk: Issuance depends on favorable market conditions, including

sufficient demand for yield-bearing instruments.

• Perception risk: Market confidence in bitcoin as collateral influences both pricing and

access to capital. Changes in perception can affect funding conditions even if underlying asset value remains intact.

These risks are not independent. They interact through market cycles, particularly during periods of volatility or tightening financial conditions.

Stress Scenarios, Limits, and Risk Boundaries

Failure Conditions

The model does not fail gradually. It fails under specific conditions:

• prolonged bitcoin drawdown combined with restricted capital-market access

• inability to refinance maturing obligations

• cost of capital exceeding bitcoin’s long-term growth rate

• loss of market confidence in bitcoin-backed collateral

These conditions are interdependent and typically emerge during periods of systemic stress.

Liquidity vs Solvency

A central distinction is the difference between liquidity and solvency.

An entity may remain solvent, with assets exceeding liabilities, while still becoming illiquid if it cannot refinance or access capital. In such scenarios, asset strength alone is insufficient to prevent distress.

Time Horizon

Time is a critical variable.

• short-term stress → high risk (refinancing pressure, volatility exposure)

• long-term horizon → increased resilience (if bitcoin appreciation resumes)

The structure is therefore highly sensitive to duration and the ability to withstand interim volatility.

Scenario Framing

Stress can be understood across three regimes:

• mild stress → higher costs, reduced issuance efficiency

• severe stress → refinancing constrained, capital access limited

• extreme dislocation → markets closed, liquidity fully dependent on reserves

The transition between regimes is non-linear and can occur rapidly.

Governance and Discipline

Structure alone is not sufficient. Outcomes depend heavily on execution.

• aggressive issuance during weak market conditions

• excessive leverage

• insufficient liquidity buffers

can destabilize the model even if underlying assumptions remain valid.

Conversely, conservative issuance, disciplined timing, and strong liquidity management can allow the structure to endure extended periods of stress.

Strategy's Bitcoin-Backed Credit Suite: Structure, Instruments, and Capital-Stack Function

Strategy’s bitcoin treasury approach evolved in distinct phases, reflecting changing market conditions and increasing sophistication in capital formation.

In the initial phase, excess cash reserves were converted directly into bitcoin. As market confidence grew and the company’s equity began trading at a premium to its underlying bitcoin holdings, capital was raised through common equity issuance, allowing for accretive expansion of the treasury.

This was followed by the use of convertible instruments, enabling the company to raise capital with minimal immediate dilution while preserving upside participation for investors.

More recently, Strategy introduced bitcoin-backed preferred and fixed-income instruments, allowing it to access yield-oriented capital even during periods when common equity issuance is less attractive.

This layered capital stack allows the company to operate across market cycles, adapting financing strategy to prevailing conditions while maintaining a consistent objective: increasing bitcoin per share over time.

Investor Segmentation

Each instrument is designed to absorb capital from different segments of traditional financial markets:

Income-focused investors → STRF, STRC Hybrid income and upside investors → STRK Higher-yield, higher-risk capital → STRD International institutional investors → STRE

This segmentation mirrors traditional capital markets, allowing bitcoin-backed instruments to fit within existing portfolio mandates.

Capital-Stack Function

Together, these instruments form a scalable, overcollateralized yield curve, allowing Strategy to raise capital without reliance on any single financing channel. Equity and convertibles dominate during periods of high market-to-NAV multiples, while preferred instruments absorb yield-seeking capital when equity issuance would be dilutive.

As bitcoin appreciates, the collateral base strengthens, improving creditworthiness and enabling continued issuance. This dynamic underpins the accretive carry while allowing the company to translate bitcoin’s monetary properties into a flexible, cycle-adaptive financing system, subject to stress constraints.

Evolution and Expansion

Strategy’s credit suite is designed as an evolving system rather than a fixed product set. The continuous introduction of new instruments reflects an adaptive approach to capital formation—responding to market conditions, investor demand, and regulatory constraints without altering the underlying objective of long-duration bitcoin accumulation.

The expanding credit suite illustrates how bitcoin is increasingly assuming roles traditionally occupied by real estate, sovereign bonds, and other real assets within the global capital stack—marking a transition toward asset-backed, scarcity-driven credit formation that is modular, exportable, and not bound to any single monetary regime.

Over time, this evolving structure begins to resemble a bitcoin-native yield curve, spanning instruments with different durations, risk profiles, and return characteristics.

From Income-Based Credit to Collateral-Based Credit

Traditional financial systems rely on the borrower’s ability to generate income to service debt. Strategy’s model introduces a shift toward collateral-based credit, where the strength and growth of the balance sheet—anchored in bitcoin—become the primary source of creditworthiness.

This transition has important implications:

• reduced dependence on operating cash flow

• increased sensitivity to collateral value

• greater emphasis on long-term asset appreciation

As a result, the risk profile shifts from default risk to market and refinancing risk.

Selected Instrument Case Studies

This appendix provides illustrative profiles of Strategy’s bitcoin-backed preferred instruments. The descriptions are intended to clarify structural differences, investor targeting, and capital-stack function rather than to catalog issuance-specific terms, which may evolve over time. Each instrument serves a distinct function within the capital stack, targeting different investor preferences, market conditions, and funding needs.

STRK — Convertible Preferred (Income + Equity Optionality)

STRK is a perpetual preferred instrument designed for investors seeking stable cash flow with conditional upside participation. It offers a fixed annual dividend alongside a conversion feature that allows holders to exchange the preferred shares for common equity once specified price thresholds are reached.

This structure mirrors traditional real estate investing, where predictable cash flows are paired with participation in long-term asset appreciation. Investors receive bond-like income while retaining upside exposure, with materially lower downside volatility than common equity.

Typical use: Deployed during periods of strong equity-market conditions, when optionality and upside participation are most valued. Capital-stack role: Deployed during favorable equity-market conditions to absorb growth-oriented capital without immediate dilution.

STRF — Cumulative Fixed-Rate Preferred (Income Priority)

STRF is a cumulative preferred instrument designed for income-focused allocators. Missed dividends accrue and must be paid before any distributions to junior securities or common equity. Within Strategy’s preferred stack, STRF carries among the highest priority for distributions.

Typical use: Best suited for stable or income-focused environments where capital prioritizes predictability and seniority. Capital-stack role: Anchors the income-oriented segment of the credit suite with maximum payment reliability.

STRD — Non-Cumulative High-Yield Preferred (Flexibility Tool)

STRD offers elevated yield with non-cumulative distributions, giving Strategy discretion to suspend payments during periods of stress without triggering arrears. This flexibility makes STRD suitable for issuance during volatile or liquidity-constrained market conditions.

In this sense, it occupies a role analogous to higher-yield segments of traditional credit markets, where increased return compensates for reduced payment certainty and lower priority in the capital structure.

Typical use: Typically deployed during volatile or liquidity-constrained conditions where flexibility is required. Capital-stack role: Liquidity and balance-sheet management instrument, trading payment certainty for issuer flexibility.

STRC — Yield-Focused Instrument with Periodic Liquidity

STRC is a yield-focused instrument with periodic liquidity features, including monthly redemption, designed to attract capital that would otherwise remain in short-duration or cash-equivalent instruments. It offers regular distributions and limited but recurring liquidity while avoiding the volatility of common equity.

At a structural level, STRC introduces competition not only within capital markets, but with the lowest-risk layer of the financial system itself. By offering yields typically above traditional savings accounts and money market instruments—while maintaining regular distributions and a familiar structure—it positions bitcoin-backed credit as an alternative to idle fiat balances.

If sustained, this dynamic has significant implications: capital that would otherwise remain in low-yield deposits or short-duration instruments can be gradually reallocated into

bitcoin-backed structures, expanding the funding base for bitcoin accumulation and positioning these instruments as competitors to the global savings layer.

Typical use: Targets capital in low-yield environments, particularly when cash-equivalent returns are unattractive, and can be deployed at scale when balance-sheet strength and market conditions support sustained issuance. Capital-stack role: Channels short-duration, yield-seeking capital into bitcoin accumulation without common-equity dilution.

STRE — Euro-Denominated Institutional Preferred (Geographic Expansion)

STRE is a euro-denominated, perpetual, cumulative preferred instrument designed for institutional investors in European markets. It extends Strategy’s bitcoin-backed credit framework beyond U.S. dollar capital markets and signals the potential for multi-currency expansion of the credit suite across different regulatory and capital-market environments.

Typical use: Supports expansion into non-dollar markets and diversification of the investor base. Capital-stack role: Expands the credit suite into non-dollar currency regimes, signaling future multi-currency issuance potential.

Comparative Positioning Within Traditional Finance

Each instrument within Strategy’s credit suite can be understood as targeting capital traditionally allocated to specific segments of financial markets:

• STRC → savings accounts, money market funds, short-duration bonds

• STRF → investment-grade fixed income

• STRD → higher-yield credit and flexible income strategies

• STRK → real estate and equity-linked income strategies

• STRE → international fixed-income allocation

This positioning allows bitcoin-backed instruments to compete within existing portfolio frameworks, enabling capital to migrate gradually rather than abruptly into bitcoin-backed structures.

Duration, Liquidity, and Risk Spectrum

The instruments span a range of duration, liquidity, and risk characteristics:

• shorter-duration, higher-liquidity → STRC

• income stability and seniority → STRF

• flexibility under stress → STRD

• hybrid income and upside → STRK

• international currency diversification → STRE

This spectrum enables more precise alignment between investor preferences and instrument design, while maintaining a common exposure to bitcoin-backed collateral.

Implications for Capital Allocation

If scaled, bitcoin-backed credit structures such as those developed by Strategy could reshape how capital is allocated across financial markets.

Rather than competing with any single asset class, these instruments draw capital from multiple layers of the traditional system simultaneously—savings products, fixed income, real estate, and cash reserves.

The key innovation lies in their ability to repackage bitcoin exposure into familiar financial formats. Investors do not need to change their mandates or operational frameworks; they can allocate through instruments that resemble existing products while being anchored in a fundamentally different form of collateral.

In this way, bitcoin-backed credit does not displace traditional finance through abrupt disruption. Instead, it introduces a parallel system that integrates with existing capital markets and gradually redirects flows toward a scarce, global monetary asset.

Corporate Bitcoin Adoption and Capital Structure Experimentation

At a broader level, bitcoin functions as a balance-sheet optimizer, increasing optionality and resilience over long time horizons. Treasury construction, rather than short-term speculation, becomes the organizing principle.

The development of bitcoin-native capital structures remains experimental. Companies are testing different combinations of equity, preferred instruments, warrants, option strategies, and debt to determine which structures can endure across full market cycles.

Not all models will prove durable. Periods of volatility will expose excessive leverage, fragile funding mechanisms, and poorly aligned incentives. Structures that depend on favorable market conditions rather than underlying resilience are unlikely to persist.

Over time, capital formation is expected to converge toward designs grounded in discipline, sustainability, and alignment between investors and issuers. In this sense, bitcoin adoption advances not through ideology, but through iterative financial experimentation.

This process is sometimes described as ‘hyperbitcoinization’—a transition toward bitcoin as a dominant monetary asset. In practice, however, this transition is more likely to unfold gradually through experimentation, capital allocation, and broader market adoption rather than through abrupt systemic change.

Second-Order Effects of Bitcoin-Based Financial Instruments

Bitcoin-linked financial instruments introduce effects that extend beyond capital formation and investor returns. These structures shape adoption pathways, capital allocation, and broader socioeconomic dynamics.

1. Market-Based Adoption Channels

Bitcoin is increasingly encountered through structured financial products rather than grassroots channels such as forums or meetups. Investors gain exposure through familiar instruments—equity, preferred shares, or fixed-income securities—integrated into existing portfolios. This indirect exposure can act as an entry point, gradually increasing awareness of bitcoin’s properties and, over time, encouraging deeper engagement, including self-custody.

2. Displacement of Speculative Pressure

Bitcoin-based income-generating instruments provide an alternative to traditional yield-seeking strategies. In fiat systems, investors are pushed into real estate and other assets to generate cash flow or hedge against inflation. By offering bitcoin-linked yield structures, these instruments can redirect part of that demand away from housing markets. This reduces the pressure that contributes to rising property prices and may, over time, ease the financialization of essential assets such as housing.

3. Reinforcement of Bitcoin’s Network Effect

Bitcoin-linked financial instruments allow investors to gain structured exposure through familiar capital-market vehicles, while directing capital toward bitcoin as the underlying monetary asset. Rather than dispersing demand across competing crypto assets, these structures concentrate capital around bitcoin itself. In doing so, they reinforce its role as the dominant monetary network by channeling incremental demand into the asset rather than fragmenting it across alternative systems.

4. Implications for Long-Term Savings and Retirement Systems

Bitcoin-backed income instruments may offer an alternative framework for long-term savings, particularly in the context of underfunded pension systems. By combining a scarce monetary asset with income-generating structures, these instruments allow savers to access cash flow without fully exiting their underlying position.
